Fix response time and follow-up consistency before spending another peso on generating
more leads.

## TL;DR

Small businesses usually have a leak, not a shortage. Leads arrive across several
channels, land in different places, get an inconsistent first response, and then get
followed up once. Automation helps in a specific order: capture everything in one place,
respond immediately, qualify before a human spends time, and make follow-up survive the
salesperson's busy week. Generating more leads is the last step, not the first.

## Diagnose the leak first

Before adding volume, answer three questions about the leads you already have.

**How long until first response?** Not average — worst case. The lead that arrived Friday
evening. The one that came through the channel nobody monitors.

**How many follow-ups does a lead actually receive?** Not the intended number. Count them
in the CRM. It is almost always fewer than the team believes, and it drops sharply after
the second attempt.

**Where do leads arrive?** List every channel. Website form, WhatsApp, Instagram DMs,
phone, referral, the personal number a client gave a friend. Then check which of those
reliably reach the same place.

Most businesses find the same pattern: leads from the main channel are handled well, and
leads from everywhere else depend on whether someone happened to notice. That is the leak,
and no amount of additional traffic fixes it.

## Capture: one place, everything

Consolidation sounds like plumbing rather than strategy, but it is the step everything
else depends on. If leads live in four places, nothing downstream can be reliable —
scoring, routing, follow-up and reporting all break in the same way.

Two things matter more than the tool: every channel is included, including the awkward
ones, and capture is automatic. A process that requires a person to copy a WhatsApp
enquiry into the CRM will hold for two weeks and then quietly stop, usually during the
busiest period, which is exactly when the leads matter most.

## Respond immediately, qualify before a human engages

Speed is the highest-leverage variable in the whole funnel, and it is mostly a staffing
problem that automation solves cleanly. An immediate, useful acknowledgement — confirming
what was asked, setting an expectation, offering a booking — holds the lead's attention
while a person becomes available.

Qualification is where the time is saved. An agent that asks the two or three questions
that determine whether this is a fit — what they need, rough timeline, whether the budget
is plausible — does the work a salesperson would otherwise do in a first call, and it does
it while the lead is still engaged rather than three days later.

We built a prospecting system for a B2B client that took this further, sourcing prospects
from Google Maps, qualifying them against their criteria, scoring lead quality, and
enriching the CRM automatically. It produced over 300 qualified leads a month and gave
back around fifteen hours a week — and crucially, the pipeline scaled without adding
headcount, because the qualification step no longer consumed a person's day.

The scoring mattered as much as the volume. Three hundred leads a month is a burden rather
than an asset if the team has to evaluate each one manually.

## Follow-up: the part that decays

First contact rarely fails. The fifth does.

Follow-up decays for the same reason payment chasing does — it depends on a busy person
remembering, and it feels low-status compared to whatever is urgent today. Automating the
sequence removes the dependency on memory and mood.

The design constraint is that the sequence must feel like a person, and must stop
immediately when the lead replies. Nothing damages trust faster than a lead answering a
question and then receiving the next scheduled template as though they had said nothing.

Design the exit conditions before the messages: the lead replies, books, buys, or asks to
be left alone. Each of those ends the sequence. This is the part most templates get
wrong.

## Reporting nobody has to assemble

If the weekly numbers require someone to build a spreadsheet, they will be built for a
month and then stop.

The reports worth automating are the ones that change a decision: leads by channel,
response time, conversion by source, and where in the sequence people drop. Channel-level
conversion is usually the one that changes behaviour, because it tends to contradict what
the team believes about which channel is working.

## Where this fits

Marketing sits alongside the other three functions in [what to automate
first](/blog/what-to-automate-first), and it is often where the loss is largest in
absolute terms while being least visible — a lead that never got a second follow-up leaves
no trace.
